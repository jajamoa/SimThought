Simulating Society Requires Simulating Thought

Position Today’s social simulations are oversimplified black boxes: demographics in, behavior out (DIBO). The paradigm must shift from behaviorism to cognitivism: (1) auditable causality behind behavior, (2) grounding in real individual experience, (3) counterfactual reasoning with consistency. The scenario. A carbon tax is announced: a price on fuel and carbon-heavy goods, meant to cut emissions, paid by everyone at the pump and the checkout. It is the classic contested policy, a climate benefit set against a cost of living, which is why what a person thinks about it matters more than which box they tick.

above: extrapolate the surface and you get three camps: yes, no, whatever. Stereotypes at scale, a society flattened into the categories the model started with.waterline
below: beliefs and experience. Behind the same label sit specific experiences that pull in opposite directions. He volunteered for reforestation in college, so he cares about the climate and wants the policy. He now runs a logistics startup and is already struggling with delivery costs, so a fuel tax lands on his margins: “this could hurt my small business.” A real individual holds both. His stance is the resolution of that conflict, and it is exactly what an intervention (say, a small-business exemption) would move.
below: extrapolate the reasoning and you get a crowd that is heterogeneous and complete: fair if transparent, who decides the rate, what is in it for me, better transit first, the rich will not care. What matters, and why

Three things a simulation must get right

1 · Auditable causality

why did the agent say that?

A stance should come with the chain of beliefs that produced it. Without a trace there is nothing to check, so a wrong answer and a right one look the same, and no one can tell you which reason moved the outcome.

2 · Grounded in a real individual

whose reasoning is this?

Beliefs come from experience, not from a demographic cell. Two people with the same label routinely hold opposite views for good reasons. Simulate the label and you get a stereotype; scale it and you get a stereotyped society.

3 · Consistent under counterfactuals

what would change their mind?

Policy questions are “what if” questions. An agent that cannot revise its beliefs predictably under an intervention cannot tell you what a policy would do, which is the only reason to run the simulation.

Together these define what the paper calls reasoning fidelity: a trace of belief formation that is traceable, revisable, and built from reusable causal parts. Today’s social simulation has none of it. The dominant recipe, demographic input to behavioral output (DIBO), is behaviorism rebuilt in software: a stimulus goes in, a response comes out, and whatever happens in between is not represented, so it cannot be inspected, tested, or falsified. Psychology abandoned that stance for a reason. We argue social simulation must make the same move, from behaviorism to cognitivism, because only an agent with an inspectable internal structure is a scientific object: something you can study, compare across people, and be wrong about. That is what makes a simulation scientifically credible rather than merely plausible.

Try it

From two answers to a belief graph you can run

The paper’s worked example, made live. Two interview answers become causal motifs, the motifs compose into one person’s belief graph, and then you intervene on it and watch a policy announcement travel through it to a new answer. Start at step 3; steps 1 and 2 show where the graph came from.

Q: How do you think surveillance might affect public safety?“It can reduce crime by aiding investigations with more transparency, which increases public safety.”
Q: Does transparency have an effect on support for surveillance?“People will have less privacy concern if they know how data is used…”

Everyday language, elicited by a semi-structured interview. No survey scale yet, no persona label. Just how one person explains their own view.

A motif is the smallest causal unit in someone’s reasoning: concept, signed arrow, concept. Each one carries the sentence it came from, so every edge in the graph can be traced back to the person’s own words. Motifs recur across people, which is what makes them comparable.

Two scaffolds

One for modeling, one for evaluating

GenMinds

a modeling paradigm

Elicit reasoning through semi-structured interviews; parse it into causal motifs; compose motifs into a causal belief network per person; run interventions on it with probabilistic updates. Symbolic structure for interpretability, a language model for elicitation and assembly. And it should know what it does not know: isolated or weakly supported nodes stay visible instead of being smoothed away.

RECAP

reconstructing causal paths

A benchmark schema that scores the reasoning, not the answer. Principles: traceability, demographic sensitivity, intervention coherence. Metrics: motif alignment between human and model graphs, belief coherence within a trace, counterfactual robustness under intervention. Every item comes from a real interview.

Neither is a fixed system. They are the minimum structure a simulation needs before “this agent reasons like that person” can be checked rather than asserted.

The call

Whether to build agents that merely sound human, or agents that reason in structured, human-like ways, is a choice the field is making right now. We argue for the latter: agents that represent beliefs, simulate causal relationships, revise assumptions, and reveal their internal logic. Causal structure is the tractable starting point, not the finish line; people also think by association, analogy, and feeling.

To simulate society faithfully, we must simulate thought.
