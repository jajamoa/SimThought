Simulating Society Requires Simulating Thought

Chance Jiajie Li1*, Jiayi Wu9*, Zhenze Mo8, Ao Qu4, Yuhan Tang5, Kaiya Ivy Zhao2,3, Yulu Gan2, Jie Fan2,7,†, Jiangbo Yu10, Jinhua Zhao4,5,6, Paul Pu Liang1,2, Luis Alonso1, Kent Larson1

tl;dr Today’s social simulations are oversimplified black boxes: demographics in, behavior out (DIBO). The paradigm must shift from behaviorism to cognitivism: (1) auditable causality behind behavior, (2) grounding in real individual experience, (3) counterfactual reasoning with consistency.

Position Align the reasoning trace, not just the output. We call this reasoning fidelity: beliefs that are traceable, revisable under intervention, and built from reusable causal motifs. What that means ↓

The problem

Demographics in, behavior out.

Most LLM-based social simulations map a persona to a plausible answer. This is behaviorism rebuilt in software: behavior as a function of external cues, with no internal state. The outputs sound coherent, but there is nothing underneath to be consistent with, nothing to trace, and nothing to update when the world changes. That is exactly where these simulations are now being used: contested policy questions such as upzoning, surveillance, and healthcare, where the reasoning is the point.

where we areBehaviorismStimulus in, response out. Persona prompts and supervised fine-tuning. Plausible, flat, untraceable.
where we argue to goCognitivismStructured internal representations. Beliefs with causes, a trace you can inspect, and a stance that follows from it.
and beyondConstructivismBeliefs continually reshaped by individual and social experience. The horizon, once the internal state exists at all.
What we ask of an agent

Reasoning fidelity, in three testable properties

Reasoning fidelity is an agent’s ability to construct, simulate, and revise a structured trace of belief formation that mirrors how people actually reason. It is not a vibe; each property has a measurement.

Traceability

how was this belief formed?

Every stance comes with the intermediate beliefs that produced it. Measured by motif-to-stance inference: given the reasons, can you recover the position?

Counterfactual adaptability

what would change it?

Beliefs revise predictably under an intervention or a changed context. Measured by belief revision under hypothetical scenarios.

Motif compositionality

does the reasoning transfer?

The same causal building blocks recur across topics. Measured by motif reuse across unrelated domains.

Try it

From two answers to a belief graph you can run

The paper’s worked example, made live. Two interview answers become causal motifs, the motifs compose into one person’s belief graph, and then you intervene on it and watch the downstream beliefs move. This is the whole position in one object.

Q: How do you think surveillance might affect public safety?“It can reduce crime by aiding investigations with more transparency, which increases public safety.”
Q: Does transparency have an effect on support for surveillance?“People will have less privacy concern if they know how data is used…”

Everyday language, elicited by a semi-structured interview. No survey scale yet, no persona label. Just how one person explains their own view.

From answer 1“…reduce crime by aiding investigations with more transparency, which increases public safety.”
motifTransparency → Crime rate → Public safety
From answer 2“…less privacy concern if they know how data is used.”
motifTransparency → Privacy concern → Opposition to surveillance

A motif is the smallest causal unit in someone’s reasoning: concept, arrow, concept. Motifs recur across people, which is what makes them shareable and comparable.

P(privacy concern)0.70
P(public safety)0.20
P(opposition to surveillance)0.69

Slide to high and the paper’s numbers appear: privacy concern 0.7 → 0.3, opposition 0.7 → 0.2. The safety path is preventive (dashed): more safety, less opposition. Illustrative weights, real structure. do() sets a value instead of observing it, so the change flows only downstream.

Ask what an average persona would do here: a “privacy advocate, 34, urban” answers the survey and the number lands wherever the prior lands. Nothing in that number tells you the stance turns on transparency, so nothing in it can predict the update. The graph can, and it shows its work.

Two scaffolds

One for modeling, one for evaluating

GenMinds

a modeling paradigm

Elicit reasoning through semi-structured interviews; parse it into causal motifs; compose motifs into a causal belief network per person; run interventions on it with probabilistic updates. Symbolic structure for interpretability, a language model for elicitation and assembly. And it should know what it does not know: isolated or weakly supported nodes stay visible instead of being smoothed away.

RECAP

reconstructing causal paths

A benchmark schema that scores the reasoning, not the answer. Principles: traceability, demographic sensitivity, intervention coherence. Metrics: motif alignment between human and model graphs, belief coherence within a trace, counterfactual robustness under intervention. Every item comes from a real interview.

Neither is a fixed system. They are the minimum structure a simulation needs before “this agent reasons like that person” can be checked rather than asserted.

Where this leads

The evaluation half is now real. HugAgent (EMNLP 2026) tests whether models recover what a specific person believes and predict how they change their mind, on 54 real participants across healthcare, surveillance, and zoning. Its headline result is this paper’s prediction: models can recover beliefs, and struggle to predict updates.

Two open problems shape what comes next. Building causal belief networks from transcripts is hard: concept boundaries, causal direction, polarity, and granularity are all ambiguous. And causality is not all of human reasoning; people also think by association, analogy, and feeling. Causal structure is the tractable starting point, not the finish line.

To simulate society faithfully, we must simulate thought.
